The core idea

Process flow → FMEA → control plan is one chain, three views of the same row. Every step in the flow becomes a row in the FMEA. Every failure mode that ranks high enough generates a control that lands in the control plan.

Process flowWhat are the steps?
FMEAHow does each step fail, how bad, how likely, will we catch it?
Control planWhat do we check, to what spec, how often, and what do we do when it fails?

The traceability test — a control in the plan that no FMEA row justifies means asking where it came from; a high-ranked failure mode with no matching control means the chain is broken. Most audit findings live here, not in the arithmetic.

Writing rows that hold up

Failure mode

A mechanism, never a part name or a purchase decision. Not "cheap eBay axle" — that's a purchasing decision. Yes "joint loses clamp load." If a row can be fixed by "buy a better one," it isn't a failure mode yet — keep asking how until you reach something physical.

Effect and severity

Rank the worst credible outcome, not the one that happened. If a failure occurred once and nothing bad came of it, that was luck — luck is not a control. Severity is a property of the effect; it does not change because an inspection was added.

Cause

The mechanism producing the failure mode. One row per cause — if two causes need two different fixes, they're two rows.

Prevention vs. detection

Prevention acts on occurrence — design change, spec change, tooling. Detection acts on detection only — inspection, gauging, test. Prevention wins; detection just tells you the failure already happened.

Scoring — RPN with two rules

RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection. Action threshold: RPN ≥ 100. It's the language most teams and suppliers speak — but it has two known holes, patched here with explicit rules rather than judgment calls.

Rule 1 — severity gate

Any severity 9 or 10 gets an action regardless of RPN. Multiplication lets a low factor cancel a high one: severity 10 × occurrence 2 × detection 2 = RPN 40, reading as trivial — but the effect is still a safety event.

Rule 2 — detection never closes a row

Only a reduction in severity or occurrence closes an action. Improving detection from 8 to 3 cuts RPN by more than half while changing nothing about the process — the failure is exactly as likely and exactly as bad.

Why these exist

AIAG-VDA 2019 replaced RPN with Action Priority (AP) — a lookup table where severity dominates and detection can't rescue a high-severity, high-occurrence row. The two rules above reproduce AP's protection while keeping RPN's familiar language.

Justifying a score change

Every S/O/D edit requires a reason before it submits, and every accepted change is logged permanently with who made it and why. A good reason names what changed in the real world, not just that you disagree: "confirmed with a torque wrench on three flange bolts, all held" beats "lowering this, feels fine now."

Common findings, in order of frequency

  1. A control in the plan with no FMEA row behind it, or a high-risk row with no control
  2. Actions closed by improving detection alone
  3. Severity lowered because an inspection was added
  4. Failure modes written as part names or purchasing decisions
  5. Reaction plan missing, vague, or with no containment scope
  6. Document not updated after a real failure or a process change
